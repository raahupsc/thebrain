What the brain is and what it does

pp. 5-6
What it is

The brain is the body's coordination and interpretation centre: a fatty pink organ made of roughly 86 billion nerve cells.

Why it matters

Everything you think, remember, feel and do runs through it, so understanding it is the foundation for the whole book.

Where it happens

It sits inside the skull, connected to the rest of the body through the spinal cord and nerve network.

The puzzle

It interprets the outside world and makes sense of it, while consuming energy effortlessly, storing memories, processing thoughts and letting you react to danger (p. 5).

The idea

The book compares the brain to several things at once. It is like a computer because it registers memories that can be quickly recalled and determines movements. It is like a vast collection of neurons because, in a sense, its main function is interpreting the outside world and making sense of it (p. 6). Information from the body moves up the spinal cord to the brain stem, then on to the cerebral cortex, which controls thoughts and memories. Neurons work like electrical circuits: signals in a logical sequence cause neurons to bond with other neurons, and this chain reaction happens incredibly fast, yet it might help you compose a symphony or recall entire passages of a book (p. 5). Neurologist Dr Robert Melillo, founder of the Brain Balance centers, is cited in the book as an expert on how the brain's structure supports these functions (pp. 6-8).

How it works

Sensory information enters, travels along nerves to the spinal cord, ascends to the brain stem, and reaches the cortex where it is interpreted and stored. Motor plans travel the reverse route. Each perception or action is the firing of circuits of linked neurons, sequenced so that the right cells activate in the right order.

Key components

  • Cerebral cortex: outer sheet handling thoughts, memories and interpretation
  • Brain stem: relay hub between body and cortex
  • Spinal cord: the information cable between brain and body
  • Neurons: the signalling cells, working like electrical circuits

Examples

Recalling a book passage

You quote a paragraph you read years ago. The chain of neurons that stored it re-fires in sequence, letting you recall entire passages almost instantly.

Mechanism: A stored neural circuit re-activates in the same logical sequence it was written in.

Reacting to danger

A ball flies at your face and you flinch before you consciously decide to. The brain's fast interpretation path, via brain stem and spinal cord circuits, drives the reaction.

Mechanism: Sensory signal, rapid subcortical processing, motor output.

Making sense of a noisy room

At a party you can still follow one conversation. The cortex weighs incoming sound against context and attention to interpret which stream matters.

Mechanism: Cortical interpretation prioritises some inputs over others.

Takeaways

  • The brain simultaneously resembles a computer, a thinking machine and a fatty pink organ (p. 5).
  • Its central job is interpreting the outside world and making sense of it (p. 6).
  • Signals travel body → spinal cord → brain stem → cerebral cortex.
  • Neurons bond in chains and fire in fast logical sequences.
  • A single circuit can support everything from symphonies to memory recall.
