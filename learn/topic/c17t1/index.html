Three learners: brains, machines, evolution

pp. 78-79
What it is

A comparison of three learning systems: neural learning in individuals, machine learning in computers, and evolutionary learning across generations.

Why it matters

Contrasting them clarifies what is special about brains.

Where it happens

Brains, algorithms and gene pools respectively.

The puzzle

Each system adapts, but on very different timescales and with different materials.

The idea

A brain learns within a lifetime, tuning synapses from experience in seconds to years. A machine learning system learns by adjusting model parameters from data, often needing thousands to millions of examples where a child needs a handful, but able to process far more data than any person. Evolution learns across generations: genes that produce better survival are selected, a process exquisitely slow but unimaginably parallel, running on every reproducing organism simultaneously (pp. 78-79). The comparison shows trade-offs: brains are data-efficient but biased by experience; machines are data-hungry but tireless; evolution is slow but world-changing. Human learning sits uniquely between: we inherit a tuned brain from evolution, then rapidly adapt it to our specific world in childhood.

How it works

Brains: synaptic adjustment from experience. Machines: parameter adjustment from data with a loss signal. Evolution: differential survival of heritable variants across generations.

Key components

  • Individual learning: synapses, seconds to years
  • Machine learning: parameters, large datasets
  • Evolutionary learning: genes, generations

Examples

A child learning 'dog' from three examples

Children generalize from a handful of dogs; an early machine system needed thousands of labelled images for similar performance.

Mechanism: Humans bring strong priors, machines must learn more from scratch.

Connects to

  • Grounds the learning mechanisms of c16; foreshadows AI-brain comparisons in c20.

Takeaways

  • Brains learn in lifetimes, machines from data, evolution across generations (pp. 78-79).
  • Brains are data-efficient; machines are data-hungry but tireless; evolution is slow but vast in parallel.
  • Human development stacks evolutionary inheritance on rapid individual learning.
