Reflexes and when the system breaks: neuropathy, Parkinson's and more

pp. 14, 18-19
What it is

The reflex arc in action, and a tour of nervous-system disorders the book lists on p. 18, including peripheral neuropathy and Parkinson's.

Why it matters

Reflexes show the system's speed; disorders show what happens when parts of it fail.

Where it happens

Reflex circuits in the spinal cord; disorders affect peripheral nerves or the brain.

The puzzle

Injury and disease disrupt signalling, producing numbness, tremor or lost control.

The idea

Your nervous system rescues you from harm through reflexes (p. 14). The complete circuit is fast because it is short: a sensory neuron detects danger, a spinal relay passes the signal across, and a motor neuron fires the muscle. The brain is informed afterwards, which is why you act before you feel. On p. 18 the book surveys what happens when the network breaks: peripheral neuropathy, where damaged peripheral nerves cause numbness, tingling or pain, often starting in the feet and hands; Parkinson's, where dopamine-producing cells in the brain degenerate, producing tremor, stiffness and slowed movement; and other conditions affecting the connective network that coordinates the body (p. 17-18). These conditions matter because they show the system's design logic: damage in the cables (neuropathy) versus damage in the control room (Parkinson's) produce very different symptoms.

How it works

Reflex: receptor → sensory neuron → spinal interneuron → motor neuron → muscle, all within the spinal cord. Neuropathy: signal conduction in peripheral nerves degrades. Parkinson's: loss of dopamine signalling in motor circuits (the basal ganglia) disrupts smooth movement.

Key components

  • Reflex arc: the fast rescue circuit
  • Peripheral nerves: vulnerable to neuropathy
  • Dopamine: messenger depleted in Parkinson's
  • Motor circuits: smoothness of movement depends on them

Examples

Stepping on a sharp object

The leg withdraws before pain registers consciously: the reflex arc's protective withdrawal response in action.

Mechanism: Spinal reflex bypassing the cortex.

A Parkinson's tremor

A resting hand trembles and movements lose smoothness as dopamine signalling fades in the brain's motor circuits.

Mechanism: Degeneration of dopamine-producing cells disrupts motor control.

Diabetic numbness in the feet

Peripheral neuropathy muffles sensation, so injuries go unnoticed: damage in the transmission cables, not the brain.

Mechanism: Degraded peripheral nerve conduction.

Connects to

  • The pain pathway (c05) shares the spinal relay; dopamine returns in c16 learning circuits and c11 sleep.

Takeaways

  • Reflexes rescue you faster than thought via spinal circuits (p. 14).
  • Peripheral neuropathy = damaged peripheral nerves, numbness and tingling (p. 18).
  • Parkinson's = degeneration of dopamine cells, tremor and slowed movement (p. 18).
  • Where the damage sits (cable vs control room) shapes the symptoms.
