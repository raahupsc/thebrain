Hypnosis: trance, suggestion and real effects

pp. 70-73
What it is

Hypnosis: a state of focused attention and heightened suggestibility, used clinically for pain and habit change.

Why it matters

It tests the boundary between mind and brain: suggestion measurably changes perception and pain.

Where it happens

Altered activity across attention and control networks.

The puzzle

Is it theatre, placebo, or a real shift in brain state? Evidence says it is a real, if variable, phenomenon.

The idea

Hypnosis is not sleep and not mind control: it is deep focused attention with heightened responsiveness to suggestion (p. 70). Brain-imaging studies show real changes in activity and connectivity under suggestion, and the effects are measurable: hypnotic suggestion can reduce the perceived intensity of pain (pp. 71-72). The book highlights cases of surgery carried out under hypnosis with reduced anaesthetic needs (p. 72), which remains rare and requires exceptionally responsive subjects, but demonstrates the effect is not mere compliance. Crucially, people differ: a minority are highly hypnotisable, most are moderately so, and a minority barely at all, which is why stage 'hypnotised' volunteers are drawn from volunteers who respond strongly. Hypnosis in animals (p. 73) covers tonic immobility-like responses such as 'animal hypnosis', an innate stillness response rather than the human phenomenon. Clinical uses include pain management, anxiety and habit change; it is not a magic erase for memory or a cure-all.

How it works

Induction focuses attention → suggestion modifies expectations and top-down control → sensory processing and pain perception shift measurably → response depends strongly on individual hypnotisability.

Key components

  • Induction: the focusing procedure
  • Suggestibility/hypnotisability: individual capacity
  • Clinical hypnosis: pain and habit applications
  • Animal stillness response: a different phenomenon (p. 73)

Examples

Dental work with less anaesthetic

A highly hypnotisable patient undergoes a procedure with reduced drug doses, pain perception genuinely damped (p. 72).

Mechanism: Top-down modulation of pain processing.

Stage volunteers

Shows select highly responsive volunteers: the spectacle depends on suggestibility, not magic.

Mechanism: Individual differences in hypnotisability.

Connects to

Takeaways

  • Hypnosis is focused attention plus heightened suggestibility, not sleep or control (p. 70).
  • Its effects on pain perception are measurable (pp. 71-72).
  • Surgery under hypnosis is rare but documented (p. 72).
  • Responsiveness varies strongly between people.
  • Animal 'hypnosis' is a stillness reflex, a different thing (p. 73).
