Why we sleep and what the sleeping brain does

pp. 47-49
What it is

The functions of sleep: restoration, memory consolidation and processing, organised in repeating stages.

Why it matters

Sleep shapes learning, mood and health, so understanding it changes daily decisions.

Where it happens

Whole-brain activity cycling through stages, involving the thalamus and cortex.

The puzzle

Why spend a third of life unconscious? Sleep is active maintenance, not shutdown.

The idea

Why do we sleep? (p. 48) The leading answers: restoration, the brain repairs itself and clears metabolic waste; memory consolidation, the day's experiences are replayed and filed into long-term storage; and protection, conserving energy and keeping us safe in the dangerous dark. Sleep proceeds in cycles: light sleep, deep slow-wave sleep where the brain synchronises and the body repairs, then REM (rapid eye movement) sleep, when the brain is nearly as active as waking, dreams are vivid, and the body's muscles are switched off to prevent acting dreams out. During REM the thalamus, which normally gates sensory information into the cortex, changes behaviour so that internally generated dream images take the cortical stage (p. 49). Skipping sleep damages exactly these processes: memory suffers, mood destabilises, and attention collapses. The practical science: the deepest consolidation happens in the night's later REM-rich cycles, which is why a full night beats a truncated one.

How it works

Sleep pressure builds during waking → sleep onset → cycles of deep sleep and REM → during deep sleep the brain replays and strengthens the day's circuits; during REM emotional memories are reprocessed → morning finds updated circuits.

Key components

  • Slow-wave (deep) sleep: restoration and memory replay
  • REM sleep: vivid dreams, near-wake brain activity
  • Thalamus: sensory gate, altered in REM (p. 49)
  • Sleep pressure: the build-up that drives sleep onset

Examples

Studying before bed

Facts reviewed at night survive to morning better than facts pulled through an all-nighter: consolidation needs sleep.

Mechanism: Sleep-dependent memory consolidation.

Emotions after a bad night

After a short night, irritability spikes: REM sleep normally reprocesses emotional memories overnight.

Mechanism: REM's role in emotional regulation.

Connects to

  • Dreams and nightmares continue in c12; consolidation underpins learning (c16); tiredness degrades illusions-resistance (c18) and IQ performance (c20).

Takeaways

  • Sleep serves restoration, memory consolidation and protection (p. 48).
  • Deep sleep restores and replays; REM dreams and reprocesses emotion.
  • The thalamus changes its gating in REM, letting dream images dominate (p. 49).
  • Later-night REM-rich cycles matter for mood and memory.
