How feelings affect the body: relax or react

pp. 15-17
What it is

The autonomic tug-of-war between the calming parasympathetic system and the mobilising sympathetic system.

Why it matters

Explains why stress changes your heart rate, digestion and breathing.

Where it happens

Autonomic nerves run to heart, lungs, gut, skin and eyes.

The puzzle

The body must switch between rest-and-digest and fight-or-flight as danger demands (pp. 15-17).

The idea

How we feel can affect our body in many ways (p. 15). When we're free from periods of stress, our body responds by reverting to a more balanced state of rest and digest: the parasympathetic nervous system at work. When we feel threatened, the sympathetic nervous system takes over: heart rate climbs, breathing quickens, digestion pauses, and blood is directed to the muscles. The book frames the choice as 'a bear or a boss?' (p. 16): whether the threat is a predator or a deadline, the fight-or-flight response prepares the body the same way, an ancient survival program repurposed for modern stressors (p. 16). As the connective network coordinates these shifts, chronic activation matters: staying in fight-or-flight mode constantly stresses the cardiovascular and digestive systems (p. 17). The practical lesson: recovery periods are not laziness; they are the parasympathetic system doing maintenance.

How it works

Threat detected → amygdala and hypothalamus trigger sympathetic output → adrenal glands release adrenaline → heart and breathing rates rise, digestion slows, pupils widen. Threat passes → parasympathetic nerves reverse the changes, restoring rest and digest.

Key components

  • Sympathetic nervous system: mobilises the body for action
  • Parasympathetic nervous system: restores rest and digest (p. 15)
  • Adrenaline: chemical messenger of the fight-or-flight response
  • Amygdala/hypothalamus: brain trigger points for the switch

Examples

Interview nerves

Before an interview your heart pounds and your mouth is dry, even though no bear is present: the same ancient program fires for a boss as for a predator (p. 16).

Mechanism: Sympathetic activation triggered by perceived social threat.

Afternoon crash after a stressful morning

Once the stressor passes, parasympathetic activity dominates and drowsiness follows as the body rebalances.

Mechanism: Parasympathetic rebound restoring rest and digest.

Watching a horror film

Heart races during the scare, settles after: a safe, controllable way the switch is exercised.

Mechanism: Brief sympathetic spikes followed by recovery.

Connects to

  • Deepens the fear response in c12 (nightmares, phobias) and links to the gut-brain axis (c04): stress and digestion talk constantly.

Takeaways

  • Feelings affect the body in many ways (p. 15).
  • Stress-free periods revert the body to rest and digest via the parasympathetic system (p. 15).
  • Fight-or-flight fires for a bear or a boss alike (p. 16).
  • Chronic stress keeps the body in mobilisation mode, straining systems (p. 17).
