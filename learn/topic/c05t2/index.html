Gate control theory, killing pain and chronic pain treatment

pp. 24-27
What it is

Gate control theory, the spinal 'gate' that can shut pain out, plus how over-the-counter painkillers work and how chronic pain is treated.

Why it matters

These are the book's practical answers to pain management (pp. 24-27).

Where it happens

The spinal cord dorsal horn for the gate; body tissues and liver for painkiller action.

The puzzle

If pain were a simple wire, rubbing a bumped elbow could not help; the gate explains why it does.

The idea

Gate control theory (pp. 25-26) proposes that a mechanism in the spinal cord acts like a gate: signals from large touch fibres, such as those activated by rubbing a bumped elbow, can inhibit the smaller pain fibres' signals before they ascend. The classic example the book uses is the stubbed toe: hopping and rubbing the toe floods the gate with touch input, reducing the pain that reaches the brain (p. 26). This is why rubbing, vibration and warmth can genuinely reduce pain. On killing pain (pp. 26-27), the book covers over-the-counter painkillers: paracetamol and NSAIDs like ibuprofen reduce pain by acting at the source of inflammation and on signalling, while stronger opioids act on receptors in the brain and spinal cord to damp the whole alarm system. For chronic pain treatment (p. 27), the emphasis is broader: because chronic pain involves sensitised pathways and psychology, treatment combines medication, physical rehabilitation, and psychological approaches rather than drugs alone.

How it works

Gate control: touch-fibre input activates inhibitory interneurons in the spinal dorsal horn, which suppress pain-fibre transmission upward. Painkillers: NSAIDs reduce prostaglandin-driven inflammation at the injury; paracetamol acts centrally; opioids bind receptors in brain and spinal cord, damping transmission.

Key components

  • Spinal gate: dorsal horn inhibition by touch input
  • Touch fibres: large fibres that can close the gate
  • OTC painkillers: paracetamol, ibuprofen (p. 27)
  • Chronic pain care: drugs + rehab + psychology (p. 27)

Examples

The stubbed toe rub

You bang your toe and instantly grab and rub it: touch input closes the spinal gate, muting the pain (p. 26).

Mechanism: Large-fibre inhibition of pain transmission.

Ibuprofen for a sprain

An NSAID reduces the inflammatory chemicals that sensitise nociceptors, so fewer alarm signals fire.

Mechanism: Blocking prostaglandin production at the tissue level.

Shaking a burned hand

The instinctive shake after touching something hot is the same gate trick: fast mechanical input competes with pain input.

Mechanism: Touch-fibre competition at the dorsal horn.

Takeaways

  • Gate control theory: touch input can inhibit pain at the spinal cord (pp. 25-26).
  • The stubbed toe and rubbing example illustrates the gate (p. 26).
  • OTC painkillers work at the injury site and on central signalling (p. 27).
  • Chronic pain treatment is multi-pronged, not pills alone (p. 27).
