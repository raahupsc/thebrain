Face recognition and prosopagnosia

pp. 32-33
What it is

The brain's dedicated machinery for recognising faces, and the condition where it fails.

Why it matters

Faces are the most socially important visual objects, so the brain dedicates special resources to them.

Where it happens

A region on the underside of the temporal-occipital cortex, often called the fusiform face area.

The puzzle

Recognising a face is not the same as recognising any object; damage reveals the difference.

The idea

We recognise thousands of faces across decades, often from brief or partial views. The brain handles faces with dedicated processing: the fusiform face area responds far more strongly to faces than to other objects. The book explains that we process faces holistically, reading the whole configuration of eyes, nose and mouth at once rather than as separate features, which is why a subtle change in eye spacing is instantly noticeable (pp. 32-33). The flip side is prosopagnosia, or face blindness: after damage to that region, or developmentally, a person sees a face perfectly well yet cannot tell whose it is, sometimes not even their own in a mirror. They may rely on voice, gait or clothing instead. The condition demonstrates that recognition is a separate brain function from seeing: the image arrives fine, the naming step fails.

How it works

Face image → visual cortex extracts features → fusiform face area matches the configuration against stored face representations → recognition and memory of the person activate. Damage to the matching step leaves vision intact but recognition absent.

Key components

  • Fusiform face area: face-specialised cortical region
  • Holistic processing: whole-configuration recognition
  • Prosopagnosia: face blindness from damage or development

Examples

Spotting a friend across a street

You recognise them in a quarter-second from a three-quarter view at distance, something few object systems achieve so easily.

Mechanism: Fast holistic matching by the face network.

Meeting someone with face blindness

They greet you by voice pattern rather than your face; the visual match never completes.

Mechanism: Prosopagnosia disrupting the recognition step.

Connects to

  • Builds on visual processing (c07); social reading of faces returns in emotions (c13) and body language (c14).

Takeaways

  • The brain has face-specialised machinery responding more to faces than objects (pp. 32-33).
  • Face recognition is holistic: whole configuration, not parts.
  • Prosopagnosia: seeing faces without recognising them.
  • Recognition is a distinct step from perception.
