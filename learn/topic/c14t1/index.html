The lying brain and body language

pp. 65-70
What it is

The cognitive machinery of deception and the physical traces it may leave.

Why it matters

Lying requires theory of mind, planning and control, so it is a window into social cognition.

Where it happens

Prefrontal cortex for planning and inhibition; autonomic system for stress traces.

The puzzle

Can you really read a liar from their body? Evidence says: carefully.

The idea

Lying is not unique to humans (p. 66): other animals use deception, from birds feigning injury to distract predators to primates hiding food. In children, classic developmental work found that lying emerges alongside social understanding: the youngest children cannot yet deceive; by the preschool years most can pass a simple false-belief task and tell a deliberate lie (p. 67). Lying is cognitively expensive: the deceiver must hold the truth in mind, construct the false version, monitor the listener, and suppress leaks, which is why the prefrontal cortex carries the load. On body language (pp. 68-70): the popular signs, gaze aversion, fidgeting, are unreliable single cues. Stress marks can indicate discomfort without guilt. The strongest sign of deception is a mismatch in detail: inconsistent accounts, not shifting eyes. The book's stance is sceptical of lie-detection folklore and supportive of evidence-based reading.

How it works

Deciding to lie → prefrontal planning constructs the falsehood → inhibitory control suppresses truthful responses → stress response may leak via autonomic signs → the listener reads imperfect cues.

Key components

  • Theory of mind: modelling what others believe
  • Prefrontal cortex: planning and suppression
  • Autonomic stress traces: unreliable cues
  • Detail consistency: the stronger evidence

Examples

The child who blames the imaginary friend

A four-year-old invents a culprit: lying appears once they can model what adults believe.

Mechanism: Theory of mind enabling deception.

Interrogation folklore vs evidence

'They looked away, guilty!' is weak evidence; contradictory details across tellings are stronger.

Mechanism: Cognitive load produces inconsistency more reliably than gaze.

Connects to

  • Uses the social brain from c13; face reading links to c08; trust and cooperation echo in c19 escape rooms.

Takeaways

  • Lying is not unique to humans (p. 66).
  • It develops with social understanding in the preschool years (p. 67).
  • Deception is prefrontal work: plan, monitor, suppress.
  • Body-language single cues are weak; detail inconsistency is stronger (pp. 68-70).
