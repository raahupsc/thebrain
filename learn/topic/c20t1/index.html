Intelligence, IQ and what they measure

pp. 89-94
What it is

Intelligence as measurable cognitive ability: what IQ tests capture, and the fluid/crystallised distinction.

Why it matters

Knowing what IQ does and does not measure prevents both over-rating and dismissing it.

Where it happens

Networks across the cortex; performance depends on the whole brain's efficiency.

The puzzle

Is intelligence fixed? What do the regions of the brain contribute? (p. 92)

The idea

The book asks what roles the different regions of our brains play in our intellect, with an 'inside the brain' map of contributions such as the frontal lobe's role in reasoning and planning (p. 92). Intelligence research distinguishes fluid intelligence, the raw processing power for novel problems, and crystallised intelligence, the accumulated knowledge and skills of a lifetime. The book relays the developmental evidence: crystalline intelligence more or less grows all life long, whereas fluid intelligence grows up to around the 20s, then flattens and later declines (p. 96). An IQ score summarises performance on standardised reasoning tasks; it predicts some real-world outcomes on average, but is a snapshot of test performance, not a ceiling, and says nothing about creativity, drive or specific talents. Sleep, exercise and practice measurably move day-to-day cognitive performance, which is the practical point of the book's closing tips (pp. 93-95): treat the brain like an athlete treats the body.

How it works

Test performance reflects processing speed, working memory capacity and knowledge access. Fluid tasks load working memory and novelty; crystallised tasks load stored knowledge.

Key components

  • Fluid intelligence: novel-problem processing power
  • Crystallised intelligence: accumulated knowledge (p. 96)
  • IQ: a standardised summary score, not a destiny
  • Frontal lobe: planning and reasoning contributions (p. 92)

Examples

The seasoned accountant

Slower on pure novel puzzles than a 20-year-old, yet formidable at financial reasoning built over decades: crystallised compensating for fluid.

Mechanism: Knowledge accumulates while raw processing gently declines.

Test-day sleep

The same student scores noticeably better after a full night: sleep restores working-memory performance (see c11).

Mechanism: Sleep-dependent cognitive restoration.

Connects to

  • Sleep in c11; learning circuits in c16; the tips operationalise plasticity from c09t2.

Takeaways

  • Fluid intelligence peaks around the 20s then declines; crystallised grows lifelong (p. 96).
  • IQ is a snapshot of standardised test performance, not a fixed ceiling.
  • The frontal lobe carries planning and reasoning load (p. 92).
  • Sleep, exercise and practice move daily cognitive performance.
